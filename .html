CT100 - Is There Any World to Come?

This project focuses on the Stratos Hyperscale Data Center, which for the purposes of this site, we will refer to as the SHDC. Not yet constructed, the SDHC is a planned data center development in Box Elder County, Utah, north of the Great Salt Lake. If fully approved and constructed, this data center could have severe effects on the environment, and the lives of Utahns. The goal of this web-project is to outline the facts and risks surrounding the development, as well as provide some perspective.

Data centers have been around for a long time now. Today, they’ve reached new heights in public discourse because the physical size and number of them has increased exponentially. Data centers are necessary for the internet and cloud services, but a new wave of data center construction for the purpose of building and training Artificial Intelligence models (which is what the SHDC will be for) has dawned upon us, and this is precisely why there is so much pushback coming from all over the place.

We don’t need city-sized data centers that raise electricity prices, pollute water supplies, and ultimately reduce the number of jobs across the country, for the main intended purpose of providing computational power for training AI models. The amount of resources these centers use, combined with the general vagueness surrounding what they do, are a couple of the major reasons why people don’t want data centers in or around their communities.

A General Overview of the Stratos Project

Cost: Reports say the figure could exceed $100B

That's billion, with a "B". One hundred billion dollars. This is coming entirely from private capital and investments, but just imagine what the world could do with $100B. With that amount of money, you could hire a million K-12 school teachers for a year, paying them 6 figures.

Size: 40,000 acres of land, with 10,000 acres of construction

To put this in perspective, all of Washington, D.C. is about 40,000 acres as well.

This is a city-sized amount of land being dedicated to a network of data centers.

Power Usage: Between 7.5 and 9 gigawatts when fully complete

For reference, the state of Utah currently uses about 4 gigawatts of energy to power the entire state. The stratos project could potentially use more than double that. In order to do so, the project would have to build on-site power generation.

So, who's pushing this project?...

The SHDC project is being led by Canadian multimillionaire investor Kevin O'Leary, and O'Leary Digital, a company Kevin is the chairman of. You might recognize Kevin from Shark Tank. He's also planning another smaller (but still not small) data center in Alberta, Canada.

Why All This Matters

The people who actually live and work in Utah do not want this data center. I learned this firsthand when I travelled there for a music festival in May, 2026. During the headliner set on the first day of the festival, a fan held up his phone with the message, "Utah says no to the data center. We don't want it." written on the screen. He was noticed by the band and their camera operator, and was projected live onto the massive screens accompanying the set. The crowd cheered, and the camera cut back to him multiple times during the set.

Data Centers Are Bad For The Environment, and For People, Too

Data centers use a lot of resources. Resources that are, inarguably, better spent on humans and other living organisms. Living organisms don't have alternative ways of surviving without water. Either we have it and we live, or we don't and we die. That's the thing with AI computational data centers though. We need information, sure, and we need research, yes. But we have been circulating information and researching for hundreds, even thousands of years without the help of AI. That is to say, there are alternatives, established ones at that, and this development is ultimately not necessary. We can live without it.

So what valuable resources do data centers use?

1. Water

You can think of a data center as a building, big or small, with a bunch of computers and servers inside. A data center can have anywhere from a few dozen of these, to thousands of them. Inside each of them is at least one processor (they can even have multiple!) Computer processors generate a lot of heat, and need to be cooled or else they can break, or run at extremely reduced performance. Many data centers use water to cool their processors, unlike how your computer at home uses a heat sink and fans, because lots of high power processors in one space can generate a massive amount of heat. Using water can allow for a quicker and more effective dissipation of that heat. You can think of this sort of like how you can cool a pan you just cooked with a lot quicker by running it under the tap than if you just let the heat dissapate into the air. Using a LOT of water is still cheaper than setting up the infrastructure to not have to use as much, so data centers just end up using a lot of water. The SHDC will do this, too. Even a medium sized data center can use up to 110 million gallons of water a year. Imagine what that figure might look like for what will be one of the biggest data centers ever built.

If you'd like a detailed explanation of how this liquid cooling process works and why it's used, you can watch this YouTube video.

Utah is one of the dryest states in the country, second only to Nevada. It doesn't get much rainfall, and the majority of its water comes from groundwater supplies and snowcaps in the mountains that melt and feed through streams into reservoirs. Needless to say, a data center that consumes millions of gallons of water each year might have some effects on both the environment, as well as the people living in Utah.

Representatives of O'Leary Digital have stated to the public that the project will not pull water from the Great Salt Lake, and that the data center will rely on a "closed-loop cooling system." If you ask me, both of these statements are just to generate buzz and make the project look better. It's like saying, "Hey everyone! We aren't going to drain the lake! Don't worry!", and, "Don't worry! The closed-loop cooling system wastes less water than an open-loop one! This is a good thing!" He has also claimed that, since the land acquired for the project used to be agricultural land, that the data center will actually lessen the water usage in the area. But let me ask you this: Which do we need more? Data centers and artificial intelligence? Or... food?

2. Electrity

As previously mentioned, if this data center is built, it will use between 7.5 and 9 gigawatts of electricity by the time it reaches fully-operational status. With an estimate that already more than doubles the current electricity usage of the entire state of Utah, the data center will require on-site power generation. This is simply due to the fact that the grid cannot support it, and it would raise electricity prices for everyone in Utah if the facility was operating using grid energy.

The initial plan for this on-site power generation was to build a natural gas plant and power the entire site off of that. Which would have raised Utah's carbon footprint by about 50%. It also would have used a ton of water, outside of the water usage of the actual data center. Luckily, local government stepped in and Stratos had to change that plan to also encompass cleaner energy generation, such as solar, geothermal, and nuclear solutions. But this does not mean that the project is in the clear.

What the Overconsumption of the Resources Could Mean For Utah

Let's start by stating some simple facts. Utah's Great Salt Lake has been drying up for decades. Water diversions upstream to support municipal and agricultural water use have played a large role in the lake's shrinkage.

Water demands as large as the ones Stratos will "need" (for what might be the biggest data center ever, as well as a natural gas power plant) will most likely have an adverse effect on Utah's existing water demand issues.

I also feel like it is necesarry to touch on the concept of a natural gas power plant being built at all for this project, even with the small win of it no longer being the plan to power the entire site using it.

This project is a plan for the future. It will cost billions. Its construction will likely take years, after years of acquiring the permits to build in the first place. Not only is it a plan for the future in the sense, but also in the sense that AI itself is going to drive the future. It is an investment. But an investment for who?

The world is running out of fossil fuels. Researchers have estimated that we will have depleted our oil and gas reserves in ~50 years. I believe that to build a natural gas plant, increasing global emmisions, for the sole purpose of powering an AI data center is already acting in bad faith. It is even worse when considering just how long we have until that plant is useless.

The fact that Stratos initially proposed that, one day, they will potentially generate 9 GIGAWATTS of power from natural gas for this project should show every onlooker that these investors do not care about the future. They care about offsetting labor costs for their own benefit, even if it means leaving the world in a state world of despair for future generations.
